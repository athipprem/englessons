Little-inversion — full reteach 🔄

Normally, a sentence goes SUBJECT then VERB: "He knew." But when a sentence starts with the word "Little" (meaning "hardly at all," "barely"), something unusual happens: the subject and the helper verb SWAP places.

Does the sentence start with the word "Little"?
↓
Normal order would be: subject + helper verb + main verb (He did know / She did realize).
↓
SWAP the subject and helper verb: "Little" + helper verb + subject + main verb. "Little did he know," "Little did she realize" — never "Little he knew."
🔑 Worked examples with FRESH verbs

"Little did the captain imagine how rough the crossing would become."
"Little did the twins anticipate the surprise waiting at home."
"Little did the crowd expect the fireworks to start early." Notice: it's ALWAYS "did" + subject + the plain base verb (imagine, anticipate, expect) — never the past-tense form.

Much vs. very much — full reteach ⚖️

"Much" and "very much" both mean roughly "a lot" — but they can't just swap freely. "Much" cannot sit directly in front of a describing-word that comes from a verb (a participle-adjective like "moved," "impressed," "startled"). In that spot, it needs "very" glued in front of it first.

Is "much"/"very much" sitting right before a participle-adjective (a describing-word ending in -ed, e.g. "startled," "convinced")?
↓
"Much" alone CANNOT go there. It needs a partner.
↓
Use "VERY MUCH" in front of a participle-adjective: "very much startled," "very much convinced." Plain "much" is fine before a comparative ("much better") or in a negative/question about amount ("not much," "how much").
🔑 Worked examples with FRESH participle-adjectives

"The scientists were very much startled by the unexpected result."
"The jury seemed very much convinced by the closing argument." Compare: "This route is much better than the old one" — here "much" sits fine, because "better" is a comparative, not a participle-adjective.

Type-1 conditional — quick refresher ☂️

This one is a single new slip — just needs one clean confirming round. Type-1 (real/likely future) pairs a PRESENT-tense "if" clause with a "will" result clause.

Talking about something realistic that might happen in the future?
↓
If + PRESENT tense, ... WILL + base verb. "If the rain stops, the match will go ahead." Never "will" in the if-half, never "would" in the result-half.

One more habit check before the test: a reversal-trap distractor means the WRONG answer choice is the near-OPPOSITE of the clue — always double-check your answer points the SAME direction as the clue, not the opposite. Same care on the easy-looking items as the hard ones.

"Although the room was completely silent, the negotiator remained implacable, refusing to change his position no matter how the other side pleaded." What does "implacable" mean here?
"Little" at the start flips subject and helper verb, on any verb. "Much" needs "very" in front of it before a participle-adjective. Type-1 conditional: present tense + "will." A near-opposite answer is still wrong — check direction, not just "does this sound related."
