Why this matters 🌍

Have Your Say and Life in Space are two very different KINDS of text — one shares opinions and debates, the other explains facts. Your school test's Reading Comprehension section will ask you questions about passages like these. Today you'll learn to spot the text type, find the main idea, answer inference questions, and work out a word's meaning from context — the four skills that show up again and again.

Before We Start — quick warm-up 🔍

Think about the two readers:

"Life in Space" explains facts about astronauts and rockets. "Have Your Say" presents different opinions about issues like homework and school uniforms. Which book is PERSUASIVE (trying to get you to think about different sides of an argument)?

Let's learn exactly how to tell the two types apart, and how to answer questions on each. →

Spotting the text type 🔎

Before you answer any question, quickly work out what KIND of text you're reading — it changes what to look for.

Skim the passage — what is it doing?
↓
Does it explain facts about ONE topic (like a science book)? Or does it show DIFFERENT opinions about an issue?
↓
Facts about one topic → INFORMATIONAL (like "Life in Space"). Different opinions on an issue → PERSUASIVE (like "Have Your Say").
🔑 Why this helps

In an INFORMATIONAL text, look for facts, numbers, and how things work. In a PERSUASIVE text, look for the "for" side, the "against" side, and what the writer wants YOU to think about. Knowing the type before you read the questions makes every answer faster to find.

Main idea vs. detail 🎯

The MAIN IDEA is what the WHOLE passage is mostly about. A DETAIL is just one small fact that supports it.

Hand-held electronic games are very popular. Many schools have banned them from the playground because they can distract children from playing and talking with friends. Some children disagree, saying the games are fun to share with others. But many people worry that children already spend too much time looking at screens instead of being active outside.
🔑 Worked example

Main idea: people have different opinions about whether electronic games should be allowed at school. A detail: "some children disagree, saying the games are fun to share." The detail is just ONE small piece — the main idea covers the WHOLE passage.

Which of these is the MAIN IDEA of the passage above, not just one detail?

Inference & word meaning from context 🧠

An INFERENCE question asks you to work out something the passage doesn't say directly, by combining two clues. A VOCAB-IN-CONTEXT question asks what a word means, based on how it's used — not just its dictionary definition.

On Earth, a rolling ball soon stops because of friction, the rubbing force between the ball and the ground. Objects in space behave very differently. Because there is almost nothing for a moving object to rub against out there, once something starts moving it can carry on at the same speed for a remarkably long time.
Two separate facts in the passage:
↓
(1) A rubbing force normally slows moving objects down on Earth. (2) In space, that rubbing force barely exists.
↓
COMBINE them → in space, objects keep moving because nothing is rubbing against them to slow them down. That's the inference — the passage never spells this out in one sentence, but the two facts link together.
Based on the passage, what does "friction" most likely mean?

Practice passage — INFORMATIONAL 🚀

Read the passage, then answer each question. Write your answer first, then reveal.

Rockets need a huge amount of fuel to escape the Earth's gravity. As the rocket engines burn fuel, they produce hot gases that push the rocket upward. Some rockets use booster rockets attached to their sides for extra power at take-off. Once the boosters have done their job, they fall away and the main rocket continues alone into orbit.

Practice passage — PERSUASIVE 💬

Read the passage, then answer each question. Write your answer first, then reveal.

Some schools let students choose their own seats in class, while others assign a seating plan. Students who prefer choosing their own seats say it lets them sit with friends and feel more comfortable. However, teachers who use a seating plan say it helps students concentrate and reduces arguments about who sits where. What do you think — should students choose their own seats?

Four reading skills locked in! 🎉

Spot the text type first. The main idea covers the WHOLE passage — a detail is just one piece. To infer, combine two separate clues. To find a word's meaning, look at how it's used in the sentence.
