Why this matters 🌍

Every day you choose between two words without noticing. "We were going to walk — instead, we took the boat." "The shop is open late; moreover, it delivers." Get the joining word right and the person listening understands you straight away. Get it wrong and they picture something that never happened. This lesson keeps four small habits sharp — the ones you have already got right on your last few tests. Nothing here is new. This is the easy half of Unit 37, on purpose: the real work is in Lesson 2, and it only needs one thing from you.

🔑 Why we still practise things you already get right

A rule you got right once can go quiet and slip away a few weeks later — that has happened before. Five minutes of easy practice keeps it there. And there is a second reason: going into Lesson 2 feeling "I can do this" is worth a lot. Easy first, hard second.

Your cloze decision tree — the same one, always 🌳

This is the routine you use on every cloze gap, in every test, forever. It never changes. Read it top to bottom:

START — look at the gap
↓
Step 1 — LEVEL. Is there a full stop just before the gap?
Full stop before it → Sentence-starter. Comma or middle of the sentence → Clause-joiner.
↓
Step 2 — LINK. What job is the gap doing?
RESULT · CONTRAST · TIME · ADDITION
↓
Step 3 — write your first choice, box it, and move on. Do not go back and erase.
What Step 1's label is actually for

A sentence-starter is a word that can begin a brand-new sentence after a full stop — Instead, Moreover, However, Therefore. A clause-joiner sits inside one sentence and glues two pieces together — because, although, while, so. Naming the level first stops you dropping a joiner into a starter's place.

Every gap you meet today is a sentence-starter — check for yourself: each one has a full stop sitting right in front of it.

🔑 Why Step 3 matters as much as the other two

The biggest cloze disasters do not come from not knowing the rule. They come from going back, changing a good answer, then changing it again, and running out of time and nerve. Decide once. Box it. Move on.

Instead vs Moreover — swap or add? 🔀

These two words look similar on the page and do completely opposite jobs. Here is the picture:

Read both sentences. Ask ONE question:
↓
Did the first thing actually happen?
↓
NO — something took its place → Instead.
YES — and something else joined it → Moreover.
Careful: Instead is not just "something surprising happened." It means one thing came in place of another. If nothing was replaced, it is not Instead.

Two more two-second checks ⏱️

Check 1 — look at the ONE word before the gap

If the word sitting right in front of the gap is a small joining word — for, about, at, after, without, of, in — then the verb after it puts on -ing. Nothing else in the sentence matters.

"praised for ___" → praised for helping. · "good at ___" → good at drawing. · "talked about ___" → talked about moving.

Check 2 — count how many, then look how long

Exactly two things? Use the -er form. The word than is your giveaway. → "taller than", "quicker than"

Lots of things? Use the -est form. The words of all or out of are your giveaway. → "the tallest of all"

How do you know if a word is "long"? Clap it. One or two claps — fast, tall, heav-y, short — is a short word, and short words take the ending: faster, fastest.

Three claps or more — im-press-ive, beau-ti-ful, col-our-ful — is a long word. Long words never squeeze an ending on the end. They put a helper word in front instead:

→ two things: more colourful than · lots of things: the most colourful of all
Never "colourfuller", never "colourfullest".

Check 3 — dress the list the same way

When a sentence lists jobs — "he feeds the cat, waters the plants, and ___ the bins" — look at the other jobs, not at the gap. Whatever ending they wear, the last one copies: empties.

How that went 📋

Carry these four into the test

1. Swap it out → Instead. Pile it on → Moreover.
2. Small joining word before the gap → the verb takes -ing.
3. Two things → -er (look for than). Lots of things → -est (look for of all). Long word → most in front.
4. A list of jobs all wears the same ending.
