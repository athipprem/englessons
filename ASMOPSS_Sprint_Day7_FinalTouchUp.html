Touch-up 1 — "so" vs "but" 🔗

so = one thing CAUSES the next thing (a result). but = the next thing is a SURPRISE — it goes against what you'd expect.

Before picking so or but, ask:
↓
Does the second half happen BECAUSE of the first half?
↓
YES → "so"   |   NO, it's surprising/opposite → "but"
"The bakery ran out of flour, ___ they had to close the shop early." Which fits?
"Coach Simon predicted the team would lose easily, ___ they fought back and won in the final minute." Which fits?

Touch-up 2 — words that don't mean what they look like 🎭

A few advanced words look negative (because of "in-" or "un-") but actually AREN'T. Always check the whole word against its meaning — don't just react to the prefix.

🔑 The test

Cover the prefix ("in-", "un-") and ask: does the word underneath actually flip the meaning, or is the whole word its own thing? A few famous ones don't flip at all.

"The map turned out to be INVALUABLE — without it, they'd have been lost for hours." What does invaluable mean here?
"This gas is highly FLAMMABLE, so it must be kept far from any flame." What does flammable mean?

Touch-up 3 — "known as" 📎

When a passage renames or labels something ("X, ___ Y, is..."), ASMOPSS almost always wants the fixed phrase "known as" — not a rewording like "famous for" or "recognized about", even if they sound close in meaning.

"The Great Barrier Reef, ___ the largest coral reef system on Earth, stretches over 2,300 kilometres." Which fits?

One quick Reading check 📖

Community Tool Library

Last spring, the town of Millbrook opened a "tool library" where residents can borrow items like ladders, drills, and garden equipment for free, instead of buying tools they might only use once or twice. Members simply return each tool within two weeks so others can borrow it next. In its first year, the tool library lent out over 1,400 items and says it has saved local families thousands of dollars combined.

What is this passage mainly about?
Why do members return tools within two weeks?
