The rule: preposition + verb = always -ing 🎯

Whenever a verb comes DIRECTLY after a preposition (words like to, on, at, about, of, for, in, with), that verb must take the -ing form — never the plain base form, never "to + verb."

Is a verb sitting DIRECTLY after that preposition?
↓
YES → that verb takes -ing, no matter what. "worried about losing," "good at drawing," "responsible for cleaning."
🔑 Why this trips people up

"Accustomed to work" SOUNDS okay out loud because "to" often signals an infinitive ("wants to work," "needs to work") — but "to" here isn't part of an infinitive, it's a preposition glued to "accustomed." The trick: check whether "to" is attached to a describing word/phrase (accustomed TO, used TO, look forward TO) — if so, it's a preposition, and -ing follows.

Memorised phrase: "arrive AT a decision" 📋

Some preposition choices don't follow a logic rule at all — they're just fixed, memorised phrases (called collocations). "Arrive at a decision" is one: the verb "arrive" is always paired with "at" when talking about REACHING a decision, conclusion, or agreement — never "to," "on," or "in."

Example: "After the long meeting, the committee arrived at a conclusion." NOT "arrived to a conclusion."

🔑 How to handle fixed phrases

You can't reason your way to a fixed phrase the way you can with a rule — you simply have to remember it, the same way you remember "arrive at a decision" is correct English. When you're not sure, picture the exact phrase as a single locked-together unit: "ARRIVE-AT-A-DECISION," not three separate words to choose between.
