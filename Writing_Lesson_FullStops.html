Why this matters 🎯

A long sentence that never stops is hard to read. The reader has no time to breathe, to picture what just happened, or to get ready for the next thing. Full stops give the reader a place to get off. Good writers use them in stories, emails, messages and speeches — short sentences make big moments hit harder.

ROUTE B · FOUR RULES

The train, as four rules

1One idea, one sentence.
2If you write “and then”, stop. Put a full stop and start a new sentence.
3Start the new sentence with a time word: Next · After that · Suddenly · Seconds later · At that moment · In the end.
4Read it in your head. If you run out of breath, cut it.

Which way do you go?

Read your sentence→Is there an “and then”, or do you run out of breath?
Yes→Full stop. New sentence. Start it with a time word.
No→Leave it. Read the next one.

Remember: a time word is a little signpost — it tells the reader when the new sentence happens.

Here is how it works on one run-on from the drill:

Run-on: We got to the park and then it started to rain and then we ran to the bus shelter.
1FIND the “and then”s — there are two.
2STOP — put a full stop in front of each one: We got to the park. it started to rain. we ran to the bus shelter.
3START each new sentence with a capital letter, and a time word if it helps: We got to the park. Suddenly it started to rain. We ran to the bus shelter.

Do the same on your ending — one “and then” at a time.
