Why this matters 🌍

You already know that after "felt," you write "felt exhausted," not "felt exhaustedly." That's one linking verb. But there are SIX common linking verbs that all follow the exact same rule — taste, feel, look, seem, sound, smell — and on the last test, the rule held for one of them but slipped on another. Today we prove the rule works identically no matter which of the six shows up, including when it's hiding inside a "find the error" question instead of a simple fill-in-the-blank.

Before We Start — quick warm-up 🔍

A reminder of something you already do well:

"After the long hike, everyone felt ___ ." Which fits?

Right — after "felt," you need the plain describing-word. Now let's check the other five verbs too. →

Six verbs, ONE identical rule 🎯

These six words all do the same job: they connect the subject to a describing-word, instead of describing an action. Because of that, they ALL need the plain adjective after them, never the "-ly" adverb.

tastefeellook seemsoundsmell

↓
Ask: is it describing a STATE (how something IS), not an ACTION (something being done)?

Use the plain describing-word (adjective) right after it — never the "-ly" form. Do this check on EVERY linking verb in a sentence, even ones that already "sound fine."
🔑 Why it slipped on the last test

"The stew tasted delicious" and "everyone felt exhausted" use the EXACT same rule — but "tasted" got missed even though "felt" was correct. That's not a knowledge gap, it's a consistency gap: the rule wasn't checked automatically on every linking verb, only on the ones already practised the most. Today changes that.

Worked examples — every verb, one at a time 🔍

taste: "The soup tasted (spicy) after Dad added extra pepper." — spicy, not spicily.

look: "The old bridge looked (unsafe) after the flood." — unsafe, not unsafely.

seem: "The puzzle seemed (impossible) at first glance." — impossible, not impossibly.

sound: "The engine sounded (rough) after the long drive." — rough, not roughly.

smell: "The bakery smelled (amazing) every single morning." — amazing (this one already looks like an adjective, so it's an easy one to trust).

🔑 The universal check

Cover up the rest of the sentence and ask: "does this word describe how the SUBJECT is, or how an action was DONE?" If it describes the subject, it's a describing-word — never add "-ly."

Now the harder version — hiding inside "find the error" 🔎

Last time, this rule broke down once it was buried inside a 4-sentence "which one has the error?" question instead of a simple fill-in-the-blank. The fix: check EVERY sentence's linking verb, even the ones that seem obviously fine at a glance — the error is often the one you'd skip past fastest.

📋 Your scan routine

For each of the four sentences: find the linking verb (if there is one), check the word right after it, and only THEN decide if that sentence is clean. Don't stop scanning after the first sentence "sounds right."

Error-scan drill — round 1 🔎

🎁 Bonus pattern — "thereby"

One more rule from the last test: "thereby" is always followed by an "-ing" word, never a brand-new subject and verb. "The bridge shortened the trip, thereby saving time" — not "thereby time was saved."

"The new filter cleaned the water faster, thereby ___ costs for the whole village." Which fits?

Quick vocab check-in — trust the direction, not the vibe 📖

A fast reminder before the test: always check your answer points in the SAME direction as the clue, not the opposite direction.

"Even after losing the final match, the whole team stayed remarkably resilient, already planning next season's training." What does "resilient" mean here?
