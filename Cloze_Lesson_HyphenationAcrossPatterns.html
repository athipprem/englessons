Why this matters 🌍

Hyphens show up everywhere in real writing — news articles, recipes, instructions, even ASMOPSS-style passages — any time a few words team up to describe something. "A five-year-old car," "a well-known author," "a two-hour delay" — get the pattern wrong and a sentence looks messy even if every word is spelled correctly. The tricky part is that the SAME words sometimes need a hyphen and sometimes don't, depending on where they sit in the sentence. Today we crack that pattern for good, across several different types of hyphens at once — not just one.

Before We Start — quick warm-up 🔍

A reminder of something you already do well:

"The museum warned drivers about a ___ (3-metre) tunnel just ahead." Which fits?

Right — the number describes the noun right after it, so it hyphenates. Today we go further. →

One rule, TWO directions 🔀

You already know that a number or description right before a noun gets hyphenated. But that same phrase can also show up AFTER a verb like "was," "took," or "lasted," standing completely on its own — and in that position, the hyphen disappears and any number word goes back to normal (plural). It's the SAME words, but the sentence position changes the rule.

Spot a number, age, or two-word description in a sentence.
↓
Ask: is it sitting DIRECTLY in front of a noun, working as ONE team to describe that noun?
↓
YES → Hyphenate the whole phrase, and keep any number word SINGULAR. ("a 2-day loaf," "a ten-year-old computer," "well-trained dogs")
↓
If NO — it's standing alone, usually right after a linking verb like "was," "took," or "lasted":
↓
NO hyphen. Number words go back to PLURAL, normal spacing. ("took two hours," "is ten years old")
🔑 Why this trips people up

It's tempting to memorise "this word always gets a hyphen" — but that's not how the rule actually works. The SAME phrase (like "ten years old") is written two completely different ways depending on where it sits in the sentence. The test isn't the word itself, it's the word's JOB in that exact sentence.

Worked examples — four different patterns 🔍

Pattern 1 — number + unit: "The tailor delivered a (three-day) suit." → describes "suit" directly → three-day.

Pattern 2 — the same phrase, standing alone: "The alteration took (three days)." → no noun right after it, so → three days (plural, no hyphen).

Pattern 3 — age compound: "The farm uses a (forty-year-old) tractor." → describes "tractor" directly → forty-year-old.

Pattern 4 — participle compound: "The centre only sends (well-trained) dogs on missions." → describes "dogs" directly → well-trained.

🔑 One check that works every time

Cover up the gap and look at the very next word. Is it a noun sitting right there? Hyphenate + singular. Is the sentence ending, or is the next word a completely different part of the sentence? No hyphen, plural.
