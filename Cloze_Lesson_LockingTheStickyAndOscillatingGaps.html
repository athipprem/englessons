Memory trick: "arrive AT a decision" 🚉

This one isn't a rule you can reason through — it's a fixed phrase (a collocation) that just has to be memorised as ONE locked-together chunk, the same way you'd memorise a phone number.

🚂 Picture it: a train pulling into a station

A decision is like a STATION. You don't arrive "to" a station, or "on" a station, or "in" a station — a train arrives AT a station. Every time you see "arrive ___ a decision / conclusion / verdict / agreement," picture a train pulling in and stopping AT the platform. Say it out loud as one word-train: "ar-RIVE-AT."

See "arrive ___" followed by decision / conclusion / verdict / agreement / plan?
↓
Don't reason — picture the train pulling in.
↓
The answer is always AT. Every single time, no exceptions.

Try it in a sentence you haven't seen before: "The two friends finally arrived at an agreement about which film to watch." Say "ar-RIVE-AT" in your head as you read it.

Subjunctive: spot the cue FIRST 🔎

The subjunctive rule itself is one you already understand: after certain trigger words + "that," the verb stays in its plain BASE form — no -s, even for he/she/it. The problem isn't the rule, it's that an older, much more automatic habit (normal subject-verb agreement, "he wants," "she needs") sometimes answers FIRST, before you've even noticed the trigger word is there.

See "that" in the middle of a sentence?
↓
STOP. Scan backwards — is there a trigger word or phrase before it? (see the bank below)
↓
YES → base form, no -s, no matter who's doing it. Decide THIS before your automatic habit gets a chance to answer by feel.
🔑 The full trigger bank — not just the ones you've seen before

Every test so far has only used ONE trigger at a time (demanded, insists, recommended). Today's practice deliberately uses ones you haven't drilled yet — the rule has to work on ALL of these, not just familiar ones:

it is essential that requested that it is vital that suggested that

Worked example: "It is essential that every passenger (fasten) their seatbelt before takeoff." → "It is essential that" is the trigger → base form → fasten, not "fastens."

Superlative: quick refresher 📏

This one just needs one clean round — the rule is already mostly solid.

Comparing exactly TWO things?
↓
Comparing THREE or more things?
↓
Two things → comparative (-er or "more"). Three-plus → superlative (-est or "most"). Longer words (2+ syllables, e.g. "creative," "memorable") always use "most," never "-est."

Worked example: "Of the five volunteers, Maya was by far the (most dedicated)." Five people being compared → superlative → most dedicated, not "more dedicated."
