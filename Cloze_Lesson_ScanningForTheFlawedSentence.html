Unit 35 proved something important: two rules you already know solidly — "arrive AT a decision" and the subjunctive — can still get missed when they show up in a NEW kind of question. Today builds exactly that second skill — the same "find the flawed sentence" move that ISA and real exams use constantly.

The scanning strategy: check ONE sentence at a time 🕵️

When four sentences sit on the page together, your eyes want to skim all four at once looking for whichever one "feels" wrong. That's exactly how a correct sentence gets missed and a fine one gets flagged by accident. The fix is a strict, one-at-a-time routine — the same "box it, don't erase" precision habit from Cloze, applied to scanning instead of writing.

🚉 Same train picture — now hunting for the ONE wrong platform

Four trains, four sentences. Three of them pull in correctly — AT the platform. Exactly one was sent to the wrong platform ("to," "on," or "in"). Your job isn't to guess by feel which train "looks" wrong — it's to check each platform sign, one at a time, until you find the mismatch.

Read sentence A only. Does it say "arrive AT" before decision / verdict / agreement / conclusion / resolution? Mark it ✓ or ✗ in your head.
↓
Now B. Then C. Then D. One sentence at a time — never compare two sentences against each other, only each one against the rule.
↓
Exactly ONE sentence will fail the check. That's your answer — even if a different sentence "sounds" more unusual for some other reason.

Worked example

A) The editors arrived at a final draft after weeks of revisions. — ✓ AT, passes.
B) The passengers arrived to the station just in time. — ✗ "arrived TO" — FAILS. This is the answer.
C) The board arrived at a resolution without much debate. — ✓ AT, passes.
D) The chefs arrived at a new recipe after several attempts. — ✓ AT, passes.

Subjunctive: a NEW shape of trigger 🔎

You've already locked in reporting-verb triggers — "suggested that," "requested that," "demanded that." Today's new shape is an adjective + "it is ___ that" — the trigger word isn't a verb about what someone SAID, it's a describing word about how important something is. Same underlying rule, different-looking cue — so the "spot the cue first" habit has to work on this shape too, not just the familiar one.

See "that" in the middle of a sentence?
↓
STOP. Scan backwards. Is the trigger a REPORTING VERB ("suggested," "requested") OR an ADJECTIVE pattern ("it is essential/vital/important/necessary that")?
↓
Either shape → same result → base form, no -s, no matter who's doing it.
🔑 The adjective-trigger bank — today's new territory
it is essential that it is vital that it is important that it is necessary that

Worked example: "It is necessary that every camper (wear) a life jacket." → "it is necessary that" is the trigger, the same family as "essential/vital/important" → base form → wear, not "wears."

Instead vs Moreover: quick refresher 🔀

Both words can start a new sentence right after a full stop — the question is what job they're doing.

Does the new sentence SURPRISE you compared to the first one — the opposite of what you'd expect?
↓
Does it instead just ADD another fact that agrees with the first one?
↓
Surprise / opposite → Instead. Extra matching fact → Moreover.

Worked example: "The forecast promised sunshine all weekend. Instead, the rain never let up." — the rain is the OPPOSITE of the sunny forecast, so it's a surprise → Instead, not Moreover.
