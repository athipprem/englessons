Why this matters 🌍

On harder, ASMOPSS-style passages, cloze gaps sometimes hide inside sentences that COMPARE two groups — like a science report saying "Group A did better than Group B." Both halves of that comparison have to use matching verb forms, like a seesaw staying level. Get this right and you can handle the hardest cloze gaps on real exam-style passages, not just everyday stories.

Before We Start — quick warm-up 🔍

A reminder of something you already do well:

"The museum recommended that every visitor ___ (book) a ticket in advance." Which fits?

Right — "recommended that" is the trigger, base form always follows. Next →

A brand-new rule — the "seesaw" sentence 🎢

Some sentences describe a study, a report, or a comparison between two groups of people or things. When a sentence says "___ MORE than people who ___," both blanks usually need to STAY IN THE SAME TENSE as each other — like a seesaw that has to stay level, not tip to one side.

See a sentence comparing two groups with "than"?
↓
Find the verb tense already used to describe the FIRST group (usually simple past: "walked," "spent," "had").
↓
Use that SAME simple tense for the second group. Don't switch to "have" partway through, and don't add an extra "who" or "they" that builds a second, disconnected sentence.
🔑 Why this trips people up

A word like "have" can look fine on its own — but in a sentence that's already telling a PAST story, switching to "have" halfway through breaks the seesaw. And adding "who" or "they" doesn't just change the verb, it accidentally creates a second little sentence bolted onto the first one, which doesn't fit the gap at all.

Worked example, step by step 🔍

"Students who reviewed their notes daily scored higher on the test than students who ___ (skip) their revision."

Step 1 — find the first group's tense
↓
"reviewed" — simple past
↓
Step 2 — match it exactly
↓
"skipped" — plain simple past, same seesaw level. NOT "have skipped" (switches tense), NOT "who skipped" (an extra "who" already exists in the sentence, don't double it).
🔑 One more check

Cover up everything except the two verbs being compared. Do they look like twins (same tense) or do they look mismatched? If mismatched, that's your answer.

A new way to practise — pencil first, screen second ✍️
