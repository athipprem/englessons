Unit 37 · Lesson 2
Why this matters 🌍

You use this sentence shape almost every day without thinking about it. "If it rains, I will take an umbrella." "If you finish your homework, we will go to the park." It is how people make plans, make promises, and set rules — a real thing that might happen, and what will happen next if it does. Today you are going to build that one shape properly, from the very beginning, and then use it fifteen times until your hand knows it.

This whole lesson is about ONE thing. Not six. Not three. One. There is nothing else to remember today, and there is no second pattern hiding at the end to catch you out.
Before We Start — a quick look at what a "half" means 🔍

Every sentence we look at today comes in two pieces, split by a comma. Like this:

If the bell rings , we will go outside.
↑ this piece starts with "if"      ↑ this piece says what happens next

In "If the film starts late, we will miss the bus" — which piece starts with the word "if"?

Good. Those two names — IF-half and RESULT-half — are what we will call the two pieces all the way through. →

Meet the two halves 🧩

Here is the whole sentence, drawn out. Look at the picture before you read anything else:

Try it on three more

If it snows, the school will close
If you press that button, the lights will change
If the eggs are fresh, the cake will rise
(bold = the IF-half  ·  italic = the RESULT-half)

Notice something already? The word "will" is only ever in the RESULT-half. Hold on to that — it is the whole lesson.

The one rule 🔑

Each half wears its own uniform, and they never swap clothes:

Say it like this: plain now-verb in the IF-half, WILL + plain verb in the RESULT-half. That is the whole shape.
Two small things about the plain now-verb

1. It has to be a whole verb on its own. Words ending in -ing — raining, leaving, dropping — are never whole verbs by themselves. They always need a helper in front (is raining, was leaving), so an -ing word can never sit in the IF-half alone.

2. The -s just follows how many. One thing doing it takes -s: the kettle boils, the seed sprouts. More than one thing doing it takes no -s: the batteries last, the seeds get, the lights go out. Both are plain now-verbs — the -s is only about how many, never about when.

🔑 Why the verb after "will" stays plain

Only one of the two words carries the work. Will already tells you this is about later on — so the verb behind it does not need to say it again. That is why it is "will go", never "will went"; "will close", never "will closed". One job, one word.

Your decision tree 🌳

Three moves, in this order, every single time. Do not skip move one — everything else depends on it.

START — find the comma and split the sentence in two
↓
Move 1 — which half starts with the word "if"?
That one is the IF-half. The other one is the RESULT-half.
↓
Move 2 — check the IF-half.
Does it wear a plain now-verb? (rings, stops, arrives)
Any "will" in here is wrong. Any past verb in here is wrong.
↓
Move 3 — check the RESULT-half.
Does it say WILL plus a plain verb? (will go, will close)
↓
Both halves pass → the sentence is correct.
Either half fails → that is your error.
Check both halves. The commonest trap of all is a sentence whose IF-half looks perfect — so you stop reading. Always finish move 3.
The two halves can swap places

Usually the IF-half comes first. Sometimes it comes second, and then there is no comma:
"The boats will return if the tide comes in early."

Nothing changes. Move 1 still finds it: the piece with "if" in it is the IF-half, wherever it sits. Then check both halves exactly as before.

The Will-Ban 🚫

This is the single mistake that costs the most marks, so it gets its own page:

Wrong → Right

❌ "If the tide will come in early, the boats will return."
✅ "If the tide comes in early, the boats will return."

❌ "If it will rain, the match will be cancelled."
✅ "If it rains, the match will be cancelled."

The sentence still talks about later on — but the word will only ever says so once, and it says it in the RESULT-half.

Three worked examples, step by step 🧭

Example 1 — "If the coach ___ early, the practice will start on time."

Move 1: split at the comma. IF-half = "If the coach ___ early". RESULT-half = "the practice will start on time".
Move 3 first, as a clue: the result half says will start — so this is our pattern.
Move 2: the IF-half therefore needs a plain now-verb → arrives.
✅ "If the coach arrives early, the practice will start on time."

Example 2 — is this one right? "If the shop closes early, the delivery would wait outside."

Move 1: IF-half = "If the shop closes early". RESULT-half = "the delivery would wait outside".
Move 2: "closes" is a plain now-verb ✓ — looks fine so far.
Move 3: the result half says would, not will. ✗
❌ Broken. The fix: "the delivery will wait outside." This is exactly the trap that catches people who stop after move 2.

Example 3 — is this one right? "If the printer will run out of ink, the posters will be late."

Move 1: IF-half = "If the printer will run out of ink". RESULT-half = "the posters will be late".
Move 2: the IF-half contains will. ✗ Stop right there — that is the Will-Ban.
❌ Broken. The fix: "If the printer runs out of ink, the posters will be late."

Practice 2 — fill the RESULT-half ✍️

Practice 3 — which one is built right? ✍️

Four sentences, one of them correct. Check each one on its own against your three moves — never compare two against each other.

Quick vocabulary habit check 📖

Not a lesson — just one habit, kept warm. When a word question gives you four meanings, one of them is often the near opposite of the right answer. It is put there on purpose, and it catches people who read the clue quickly and then pick by feel.

The habit: find the clue in the story, then check your answer points the SAME way as the clue — not the opposite way.
"Because the shelf kept wobbling, the carpenter was painstaking about every screw, tightening each one and testing it twice." What does "painstaking" mean here?
"Even after losing three times, Ploy remained undaunted, signing up for the next competition the very same evening." What does "undaunted" mean here?

How that went 📋

The whole lesson, in three lines

1. Split at the comma. The piece with "if" is the IF-half.
2. IF-half wears a plain now-verb. Never "will". Never the past.
3. RESULT-half wears WILL + a plain verb. Check this half too — do not stop after the first one.

That is all of it. One pattern, fifteen goes, every one finished on a correct answer. The test has seven questions on this exact shape and nothing else new — you have already done more than twice that many. Go and get them.
