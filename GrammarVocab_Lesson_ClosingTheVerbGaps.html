Why this matters 🌍

Last unit's test found something precise: the linking-verb rule ("smelled strong," not "smelled strongly") works perfectly when you're spotting someone ELSE's mistake — but it slipped on two verbs (smell, sound) when YOU had to fill in the blank yourself, cold. Today closes that exact gap, plus two other small habits that slipped: the "Little did he know" word-order flip, and "very much" needing "very" in front of it.

🔑 Today's precise targets

smell and sound get a confirming rep in the exact format that tripped last time: filling in the blank yourself, no options to compare against first. Plus "Little did he know" and "very much" — both worked before, both slipped once last test, both get one more clean rep today.

Worked examples — smell and sound, fill-in style 🔍

smell: "The garage smelled (musty) after the rainstorm." — musty, not mustily.

sound: "The choir sounded (rich) in the stone chapel." — rich, not richly.

Filling in a blank right after "smelled" or "sounded"?
↓
Same question as always: is it describing a STATE (how something IS), not an action?
↓
Then it's the SAME rule as every other linking verb: plain describing-word, never "-ly." Don't let a new verb make you doubt a rule you already know.

Two quick habits — one more clean rep each 🔁

"Little" inversion: when a sentence starts with "Little," the subject and helper verb swap places — "Little did he know," never "Little he knew."

3. Which sentence is correctly written?

"Much" vs "very much": "much" alone can't sit in front of a participle-adjective like "impressed" or "relieved" — it needs "very" first.

4. "The judges were ___ impressed by the final performance." Which fits?

Quick vocab reminder 📖

One more habit check before the test: a reversal-trap distractor means the WRONG answer choice is the near-OPPOSITE of the clue — always double-check your answer points the SAME direction as the clue.

"Even though he'd trained for months, the runner felt surprisingly resolute at the starting line, refusing to let his nerves show." What does "resolute" mean here?
Smell and sound follow the exact same rule as every other linking verb — a new verb doesn't mean a new rule. Little did he know, very much relieved — both are one clean rep away from fully locked in.
