Type-1 conditional — full re-teach ☂️

Type-1 describes something REAL and LIKELY in the future: "If the rain stops, the match will go ahead." The mix-up usually happens because Type-1 gets tangled up with Type-2 (a more IMAGINED, less likely idea about NOW): "If I won the lottery, I would travel the world." Both are valid — the trap is mixing their halves together.

Look at the RESULT half FIRST. Does it say "will"?
↓
"will" → this MUST be Type-1 → the if-half MUST be present tense (opens, stops, rains). Plain "would" (no "have") → this MUST be Type-2 → the if-half MUST be past simple (opened, stopped, rained).
↓
Do the two halves match the SAME row? If yes, correct. If the if-half is present tense but the result says "would" — or the if-half is past tense but the result says "will" — that's the error.
🔑 One extra check: the Will-Ban

The if-half itself NEVER uses "will," even in Type-1: "If the tide will come in early" is always wrong — it should be "If the tide comes in early." "Will" only ever appears in the RESULT half.

🔑 Worked examples, side by side

Type-1 (real, likely): "If the museum opens early, the tour will start on time."
Type-2 (imagined, less likely): "If the museum opened early, the tour would start on time."
Notice: every word in each sentence matches ITS OWN row — never a present if-half paired with "would," and never a past if-half paired with "will."

Much / very much — spotting it in a sentence 🕵️

You already know the rule for writing your own sentence. Now it's time to use it to check someone ELSE's sentence — using the same one-at-a-time scanning routine from Lesson 1.

Read sentence A. Find any word "much" or "very much." Is it sitting right before a participle-adjective (a feeling word ending in -ed, like "delighted," "astonished," "relieved")?
↓
If YES — is "very" glued in front of "much"? If "much" is alone, that sentence FAILS the check.
↓
Repeat for B, C, D — one at a time. Exactly one sentence will fail. Remember: "much" alone is FINE before a comparative ("much faster," "much better") — only participle-adjectives need "very much."
🔑 Worked example

A) The crowd was very much delighted by the fireworks display. — ✓ has "very," passes.
B) The critics were much astonished by the unexpected twist. — ✗ "much astonished" needs "very" — FAILS. This is the answer.
C) This laptop is much faster than the old one. — ✓ "much" before a comparative is fine, passes.
D) The neighbors weren't much troubled by the noise. — ✓ this is a negative-amount use, not a participle-adjective pairing, passes.

Little-inversion — the exact verb form after "did" 🔍

The flipped order itself is already solid: "Little" + "did" + subject. Today's fine-tune is about what comes right AFTER the subject — that verb must stay in its plain BASE form, because "did" is already carrying the past tense for the whole sentence.

"Little did [subject] ___" — what goes in the blank?
↓
Always the BASE form — no "-ed," no "-s." "Did" already shows it happened in the past, so the main verb doesn't need its own past-tense ending too.
🔑 Correct vs. incorrect, side by side

✅ "Little did she anticipate the trouble ahead." (base form: anticipate)
❌ "Little did she anticipated the trouble ahead." (wrong — "anticipated" is already past tense, and "did" is doing that job)

Meet Your New Skill: Figurative Language!

Figurative language is when words paint a picture instead of just stating a plain fact. Two of the most common kinds are the simile and the metaphor.

🔑 Simile — compares using "like" or "as"

"The wind howled like a wolf." "Her smile was as bright as sunshine." A simile always keeps "like" or "as" as the connecting word.

🔑 Metaphor — says one thing simply IS another

"The classroom was a zoo during recess." "The old car was a dinosaur, wheezing up every hill." No "like" or "as" — the sentence just states it directly, as if it were literally true.

This is just a first look — no pressure, nothing scored yet. Over the next few units you'll get more practice spotting and using these, building up toward your first real scored check later on.
